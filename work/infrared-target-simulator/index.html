Infrared target simulator

A prototype that reproduces thermal signatures for testing FLIR cameras: a resistive heater array, MOSFET-driven channels, and Arduino firmware that switches heating patterns across the array under PWM control.

Embedded Systems Engineering Intern. I built the prototype under the guidance of a senior engineer while learning PWM and heater control.

Problem and constraints

Testing a thermal camera needs a target whose infrared signature is known and repeatable. A resistive heater array can provide one: each element is driven to a chosen power, the array as a whole presents a spatial thermal pattern, and the camera's image of it can be compared against what was programmed.

The prototype had to switch heating patterns across the array under software control, which means one independently driven channel per element, a driver stage that can carry heater current from a logic-level signal, and firmware that sets each channel's duty.

What I built

  • Heater-control firmware. Arduino code that holds a heating pattern and drives it onto the array as per-channel PWM duty cycles, switching between patterns over time.
  • Driver channels. MOSFET-driven heater channels, assembled and tested on the bench under a senior engineer's guidance. This is where I learned multi-channel switching and driver-circuit fundamentals in practice: gate drive from a logic-level output, heater current through the switch, and checking each channel before trusting the array.
  • Thermal validation. Programmed states captured with a FLIR thermal camera and compared against the intended pattern.
  • Scalability draft. An analysis of how a 4×4 or 8×8 array architecture could extend to a larger grid, weighing power distribution against channel synchronisation.

Decisions and what I learned

The engineering here was mostly practical. PWM gives proportional control of a resistive element's average power from a digital output, and because a heater's thermal time constant is long compared with the PWM period, the element integrates the pulses into a steady temperature. A MOSFET per channel keeps the heater current off the microcontroller and lets one logic line switch a much larger load.

The scope was deliberately bounded. I built and tested under guidance rather than owning the design; the senior engineer set the architecture and checked the work. What I took away is the hands-on competence the résumé lists under embedded and hardware: Arduino firmware, PWM control, MOSFET driver and switching circuits, breadboard prototyping and soldering, and using a thermal camera as a measurement instrument.

Validation

Validation was qualitative and direct: program a heating pattern, capture the array with the FLIR camera, and compare the thermal image against the programmed state. No performance metrics (uniformity, rise time, contrast) were recorded for publication, and none are stated here.

Scalability analysis

Separately from the implemented prototype, I drafted an analysis of how a 4×4 or 8×8 array architecture could extend to a larger grid. The two trade-offs it considers are power (total and per-channel current, distribution and switching losses as the element count grows) and synchronisation (keeping many PWM channels coherent so a pattern appears as one thermal image rather than a set of independently timed elements). This was a proposal on paper, not a second build.

Limitations and scope

  • This was a prototype built under supervision on a defence project. Photographs, schematics, dimensions, the channel count and any measured figures cannot be published, which is why the only visual on this page is a conceptual diagram.
  • The scalability analysis was a draft; the larger grid was not built.
  • The internship lasted one month (January 2026), so the depth is that of a focused first hardware project, not a completed product.
